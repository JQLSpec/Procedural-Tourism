# Procedural Tourism: A Cooperative Travel Game
## Game Design Document

**Version 1.2 · Design baseline for playtesting**

*Branding revision: official title adopted; gameplay and card identifiers unchanged.*

This is a reference for designers and expansion authors, not a player rulebook. The official game title is Procedural Tourism: A Cooperative Travel Game. The design baseline supersedes older references to 24-hour rounds and failure-triggered curses.

## 1. Vision and experience

A low-key, cooperative travel adventure that uses randomly drawn objectives and constraints to help travelers discover places, learn about cultures, and step outside their usual routines. The destination is the game board; the experiences are the reward.

The intended audience is adult travelers, initially two companions, with support for larger groups subject to playtesting. Play can be inserted into an existing vacation without replacing its itinerary. Players need not be equally adventurous, knowledgeable, mobile, or comfortable with technology.

### Design pillars

- **Discovery over competition:** Reward firsthand encounters, curiosity and learning rather than speed, distance, points or domination.
- **Cooperation through asymmetry:** Give each traveler a different purpose and limitation, with a shared rule that creates opportunities to help each other.
- **Meaningful constraints:** Change everyday decisions, not merely add errands or busywork.
- **Low stakes:** Failure is an acceptable outcome and never increases future difficulty.
- **Vacation first:** Respect rest, commitments, budgets, personal boundaries and accessibility.
- **Emergent adventure:** Simple cards should combine into varied experiences without requiring elaborate scenarios or a game master.

Non-goals include extreme physical challenges, competitive racing, mandatory stranger participation, expensive purchases, public embarrassment, compulsory social media and exhaustive recordkeeping.

## 2. Core architecture

| Element | Quest | Condition | Curse |
|---|---|---|---|
| Function | What to accomplish | Shared rules of the adventure | Personal complication |
| Draw | One per traveler | One per group | One per traveler |
| Target core deck | 60 | 40 | 50 |
| Categories | 6 | 9 | 5 |
| Scope | Individual objective | Collective | Individual effect |

A round lasts **12 consecutive hours** from a mutually agreed start time. Rounds are opt-in and need not be played every day. Draw quests first, the condition second, and curses third; resolve conflicts before pursuit. Two travelers therefore begin with five cards. Players may combine compatible quest activities, work separately by agreement, and take breaks. Each quest holder remains responsible for meaningful participation and demonstrating completion.

At round end, evaluate quest completion and expire all remaining conditions and curses. No stacking, carryover or failure-triggered curses. A proposed baseline for deck management is drawing without replacement within a session/trip, setting used and exchanged cards aside, and reshuffling that deck's discard pile when exhausted. Keep active cards out of the shuffle. No formal scoring system is needed for initial playtesting.

## 3. Shared design constraints

- Safety, local law, access restrictions, health, accessibility, dietary needs, consent, privacy, essential communication and existing travel/work commitments override game effects without penalty.
- Agree a modest spending limit and choose feasible activities. No card should require substantial spending or reliance on unwilling participants.
- Use objective, observable requirements and plain language. Do not require proof beyond reasonable evidence such as notes, photos where permitted, maps, tickets or an oral account.
- Maps, translation, internet research and AI are allowed unless a card restricts them. Research supports, not replaces, firsthand experience.
- Cards should be playable with ordinary travel equipment and light physical effort. Provide alternatives for predictable access barriers.
- Never require trespass, photographing people without appropriate consent, disrespectful conduct at sacred sites, or activity that creates a burden for local workers.
- Avoid designing around an assumption that all players drink alcohol, eat particular foods, drive, own smartphones, speak the local language, or have unrestricted mobility.

## 4. Quest design

Quests are the primary content. They should create opportunities for firsthand exploration and an identifiable learning outcome. A discovery should often be the beginning of an investigation, not the entire task.

| Category | Core cards | Share | Design focus |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Exploration | 15 | 25% | Unfamiliar districts, public spaces, geography and everyday infrastructure |
| History | 10 | 16.7% | Local stories, historical change, industry and heritage |
| Culinary | 10 | 16.7% | Food traditions, ingredients, markets and regional dishes |
| Cultural | 10 | 16.7% | Language, etiquette, crafts and community life |
| Creative | 8 | 13.3% | Photography, writing, observation and interpretation |
| Journey | 7 | 11.7% | Transportation, routes and the experience of getting there |
| **Total** | **60** | **100%** | |

Base quests require at least 90 minutes of meaningful effort and should be achievable within 240 minutes, including relevant local travel, research and documentation. If requirements finish early, deepen the investigation rather than pad time. Estimates are planning targets, not automatic failure deadlines. Avoid single-location tasks that can be finished in minutes and tasks dependent on rare events or booking availability. Activities performed before drawing do not count. Ordinary sightseeing can count if it meets every requirement.

**Card schema:** ID; title; category; estimated effort; objective; measurable completion requirements; optional flavor text; access or substitution notes when necessary.

**Example:** End of the Line (Journey): travel to an unfamiliar transit terminus, explore for at least 90 minutes, discover three points of interest and explain them. Plan for 2–4 hours total. A transit-poor destination should offer an equivalent locally appropriate quest or permit an exchange.

## 5. Condition design

Conditions shape how the entire group experiences the destination. They are not extra quests and need not be disadvantages. Each should change one meaningful dimension of play without routinely pushing base quests beyond four hours.

| Category | Core cards | Share | Design focus |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Transportation | 6 | 15% | Modes, routes, service types |
| Navigation & Geography | 6 | 15% | Spatial limits, unfamiliar areas, navigation approaches |
| Economics & Consumption | 4 | 10% | Spending and business selection |
| Technology & Information | 5 | 12.5% | Search, reviews, devices and information sources |
| Language & Social Interaction | 4 | 10% | Communication and etiquette |
| Food & Dining | 3 | 7.5% | Meal choices and local dining habits |
| Time & Pace | 4 | 10% | Round-relative timing, breaks and lingering |
| Cooperation & Decision-Making | 4 | 10% | Shared roles and coordination |
| Chance & Serendipity | 4 | 10% | Random choices and spontaneous detours |
| **Total** | **40** | **100%** | |

Mechanical types are an independent classification across the same 40 cards: 20 restrictive (50%), 14 prescriptive (35%), and 6 beneficial (15%). Beneficial cards grant flexibility, such as a redraw, equivalent requirement substitution or curse suspension. Do not introduce persistent rewards without defining a separate mechanic.

Every condition specifies either **Full Round** (the entire 12-hour window) or **Quest Activities** (active planning, pursuit and associated travel). Prefer quest scope for potentially disruptive constraints. Use relative timing rather than fixed clock times where possible.

**Card schema:** ID; title; category; mechanical type; scope; explicit rule; optional flavor text and exceptions.

**Example:** Unreviewed (Technology & Information; restrictive; quest activities): no online reviews, ratings or rankings for destination selection. Factual opening hours, access and safety information remain available.

## 6. Curse design

Curses are mandatory personal complications every round, not punishments. Favor entertaining asymmetric limitations that let companions compensate for one another. A curse should add at most approximately 30 minutes of effort, including any optional redemption, and preferably keep the combined outing within four hours where feasible. The 30 minutes is a design ceiling target, not a required extra task.

| Category | Core cards | Share | Design focus |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Mobility & Navigation | 10 | 20% | Personal movement or route limitations |
| Information & Resource Deprivation | 10 | 20% | Temporary loss of conveniences |
| Quest Complications | 10 | 20% | Modest extra or modified completion criteria |
| Dependency & Loss of Autonomy | 10 | 20% | Limited delegation of game decisions |
| Chaos & Compulsions | 10 | 20% | Whimsical obligations and controlled randomness |
| **Total** | **50** | **100%** | |

Duration types: **Persistent** (lasts until round end), **Conditional** (can end early through a specified redemption), and **Quest-bound** (ends upon quest completion). Scope is separately specified as full round or quest activities. All expire no later than round end. Only one active curse per traveler; curses cannot be transferred. Assistance is allowed unless prohibited and must respect the helper's own curse and shared condition. Delegated decision authority never includes personal boundaries, essential expenses or safety.

**Card schema:** ID; title; category; effect; duration type; scope; optional redemption; exceptions or compatibility notes; optional flavor text.

**Example:** The Accidental Tourist (Mobility & Navigation; persistent; quest activities): the holder may not personally consult maps or navigation apps; a companion can navigate. Essential navigation remains exempt.

## 7. Interaction and adjudication

Design cards to compose, not cancel or deadlock. Test the following resolution order:

1. Apply universal exemptions; no penalty for essential needs.
2. Exchange an intrinsically infeasible quest (closures, geography, access or other objective barriers).
3. If the condition conflicts with a feasible base quest, redraw the shared condition.
4. If a curse duplicates the condition, removes a necessary resource, or makes a quest unreasonable, redraw that curse. If two otherwise valid curses are jointly incompatible, agree which to redraw or choose randomly.
5. Recheck after every exchange. Mere inconvenience is not a reason to redraw. Apply the same procedure if circumstances change mid-round; preserve valid progress where requirements still match.

An explicit beneficial card may override the ordinary restriction it names, but never universal safeguards. Companions may help only within their own restrictions. If no feasible combination remains, pause or end the round rather than force play.

Round outcomes: Successful Expedition (all quests complete while observing applicable effects), Partial Expedition (some but not all), Misadventure (none). No penalties carry forward. An optional expedition journal records outcomes and discoveries. Proposed adjudication default: a material uncorrected card breach makes the affected quest unsuccessful; an accidental breach that can be remedied should be corrected in good faith. A shared breach affects only participating travelers responsible for it, unless the card explicitly requires a collective result.

## 8. Expansion and destination edition guidelines

### Product models

| Model | Purpose | Integration |
|---|---|---|
| Core expansion | More reusable adventures | Add compatible cards to existing decks |
| Destination pack | Local flavor and knowledge | Substitute or mix destination-tagged cards into each corresponding deck |
| Destination edition | Standalone localized game | Supply all three playable decks and the same core rules |
| Thematic module | Focus on a travel style, e.g. rail, food or architecture | Optional curated subset; preserve category variety where practical |

Do not create a new mandatory deck or change round length, draw structure or failure mechanics merely to localize content. Label intentional rule variants separately. Keep core category ratios as initial targets for general-purpose editions; thematic packs can intentionally skew them, with a stated rationale. When mixing packs, remove incompatible cards before drawing; do not require players to purchase another pack to resolve a card.

### Localizing content

Use real, locally meaningful practices, architecture, transit, geography, language and history. Prefer classes of places with several substitutes over a single named attraction. When named sites, opening times, fares or regulations matter, verify them at publication and record sources, date checked, seasonal constraints and fallback options. Do not stereotype a culture or treat residents as props. Seek local review for language and etiquette. Include translations where useful, and allow the player to use respectful alternatives.

Example Japan-oriented adaptations (illustrative, not verified venue recommendations):

| Deck | Localized concept | Design rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Quest | Station Neighborhoods: compare two ordinary station districts and investigate how they serve residents | Retains Journey/Exploration effort and learning |
| Quest | Regional Foodways: investigate a local specialty through a market and a meal | Culinary experience with dietary substitutes |
| Condition | Local Lines: use ordinary local services for quest travel where a practical alternative exists | Transportation constraint without requiring a specific rail line |
| Curse | The Station Navigator: companion selects the viable exit at unfamiliar stations | Personal dependency, not a restriction on essential navigation |

For rural, island or car-oriented destinations, substitute scenic routes, ferries, public trails or village centers as appropriate; avoid assuming metropolitan transit. For accessibility-focused editions, use equivalent learning and decision-making rather than reduced dignity or punitive exceptions.

### Authoring and release workflow

- Identify the intended discovery or decision before writing a card.
- Assign category, scope, type and effort; allocate a stable ID using the registry and convention in Section 9 (e.g. `CORE-Q-EX-01` or `JPN-Q-JO-01`).
- Specify prerequisites and fallback options; screen for cultural, safety and accessibility issues.
- Check interactions with every category and sample actual cards, especially resource bans, reciprocal dependencies, timing, transport, and evidence requirements.
- Playtest in unfamiliar urban and smaller-town settings, with different budgets and mobility needs. Record actual active time, added effort, spend, redraw reasons, cooperation, enjoyment and discoveries.
- Revise cards causing frequent redraws, trivial completion, excessive effort or conflict. Preserve version history; identify replaced cards and rules.

Suggested release gates: no unresolved safety or logical conflicts in tested combinations; base quests typically fit 90–240 minutes; curse overhead typically stays within 30 minutes; conditions do not become separate errands; participants report meaningful discoveries and a manageable vacation pace. Collect evidence before setting numeric pass-rate thresholds.

## 9. Card identifiers and namespace registry

### Canonical format

Every card in every deck, expansion and destination edition must have a globally unique, stable identifier:

`<SET>-<DECK>-<CATEGORY>-<NUMBER>`

| Component | Convention |
|---|---|
| SET | Registered uppercase alphanumeric namespace, beginning with a letter; CORE is reserved for the original game. Use a distinct namespace for each separately authored expansion or edition. |
| DECK | Q = Quest; C = Condition; U = Curse. These codes are reserved. |
| CATEGORY | Registered two-letter uppercase code, interpreted within its deck. |
| NUMBER | Positive sequential integer within the set/deck/category combination, padded to at least two digits (01–99, then 100 and beyond). 00 is reserved. |

Syntax validation: `^[A-Z][A-Z0-9]*-[QCU]-[A-Z]{2}-[0-9]{2,}$`. In addition to syntax, validate registry membership, positive canonical numbering and uniqueness. Do not encode language, revision, difficulty or card title in the identifier.

### Set namespaces and expansion examples

| Namespace | Status / intended use |
|---|---|
| CORE | Allocated: original 150-card core game |
| JPN | Proposed: Japan destination pack or edition |
| ITA | Proposed: Italy destination pack or edition |
| JPN02 | Illustrative: a second distinct Japan expansion |
| RAIL01 | Illustrative: a rail-themed module |

Only CORE is currently allocated. Proposed and illustrative namespaces do not represent released content. Maintain a central set registry recording namespace, product title, model, destination/theme, owner and release status. Check it before assigning a new namespace; do not assume country codes alone distinguish multiple releases. A standalone edition that reuses an unchanged core card retains its CORE identifier.

Example identifiers for hypothetical Japan content: `JPN-Q-JO-01` (Journey quest), `JPN-Q-FD-01` (Culinary quest), `JPN-C-TR-01` (Transportation condition), `JPN-U-MN-01` (Mobility curse). These are examples, not authored cards.

### Identity, revision and migration rules

- Allocate the next unused number in each set/deck/category sequence. Gaps are acceptable; never recycle a retired identifier or renumber cards to close gaps.
- Preserve an ID through copy edits, translations, accessibility wording and balance adjustments that retain the card's fundamental identity. Track card revision and document version separately.
- A substantively new or replacement card receives a new ID; record the predecessor and successor. If reclassification requires a different category code, retire the old ID and record its successor instead of silently changing it.
- Reprints and localized translations of the same card retain the original ID. Distinct destination adaptations with changed objectives or effects receive their own set ID.
- Maintain a manifest of IDs, titles, categories, revisions, aliases and retirement status. Validate all three decks together before release, including mixed expansion manifests; deduplicate identical card identities unless a product explicitly specifies duplicate copies.
- The Quest Deck v1.1 migration preserves every original number and maps E→EX, H→HI, F→FD, U→CU, A→CR and J→JO under the CORE-Q namespace. For example, E01 becomes CORE-Q-EX-01 and J07 becomes CORE-Q-JO-07. Resolve old journal references through these aliases; do not treat legacy and canonical IDs as separate cards.
- The Conditions and Curse Decks v2.0 already use canonical IDs and require no migration.

## 10. Status and open questions

All 150 core cards have been authored as first playable drafts: 60 quests (v1.2), 40 conditions (v2.1) and 50 curses (v2.1). All use canonical identifiers. Field playtesting and combination testing remain pending. Evaluate the curse duration mix, larger-group support, discard procedure and compliance adjudication. Consider optional campaign records or rewards only after the cooperative loop is proven enjoyable.
